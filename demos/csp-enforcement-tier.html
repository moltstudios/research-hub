Content Security Policy: the enforcement tier

Our catalog had Trusted Types — the DOM-XSS sink wall. It had zero coverage of the policy itself: the directive grammar, the delivery rules, the nonce/hash precedence, and the reporting tier that tells you when a policy is actually breaking your app in production.

CSP is the one security control that breaks things. You ship a policy, a third-party script silently stops loading, your analytics vanish, and nothing tells you — because the reporting directive you added is the one the browser quietly ignores.

Everything below is measured. 231 probe lanes across Chrome-for-Testing 155, Brave 154 and WebKit 26.5, plus the live lab in §1, which re-runs the laws in your browser, right now.

The sandbox carries allow-scripts allow-same-origin: a sandboxed frame is an opaque origin, and an opaque-origin frame's window is unreadable from the parent (SecurityError) — so without allow-same-origin the lab could not read its own results. The sandboxed document carries no network or storage capability of its own: its policy is set inside its own srcdoc, and it can neither reach this page's origin nor submit a form.
THE VIOLATION-EVENT LAW. securitypolicyviolation fires on the window whose document was blocked, and the event carries the directive that fired, the blocked URL, the disposition (enforce or report), the sourceFile, the line and column, and a sample of the offending code.
  • effectiveDirective is the fallback directive that actually applied (e.g. script-src-elem when only script-src was written) — not the one you wrote
  • the browser console logs the same violation as an error, and the console text names the real directive
  • a violation for a blocked resource is fired once per resource, not once per policy

2 · The delivery law — a header policy and a meta policy do not merge

Three delivery routes exist: the Content-Security-Policy header, a <meta http-equiv> tag, and the Content-Security-Policy-Report-Only header (which reports but never blocks). They combine by intersection, not by union — and the meta route has a hard positional window.

DeliveryWhat the browser appliedVerdict
THE META-WINDOW LAW. A <meta http-equiv="Content-Security-Policy"> is honoured only if it appears before the resource it is meant to govern. Measured: a meta policy in <head> blocked every script; the same policy placed after the scripts in <body> blocked nothing — 0 violations, all six probes ran. *A late meta is not a weaker policy. It is no policy at all.*
THE INTERSECTION LAW (headline). A header policy and a meta policy are enforced as a conjunction. Measured:
  • header script-src 'none' + meta script-src 'self' → everything blocked. The meta's looser 'self' does not rescue anything.
  • header script-src 'self' + meta script-src 'none' → everything blocked. The meta's tighter 'none' wins over the header's looser 'self'.
*The tighter policy always wins, and the meta can tighten a header but never loosen it. A meta tag is a floor, not a ceiling.*
THE META-LIMITATION LAW. frame-ancestors, report-uri and sandbox are ignored in a meta tag — the spec makes them header-only. Measured: <meta http-equiv="CSP" content="frame-ancestors 'none'"> produced 0 violations and no framing protection. *If you are defending against clickjacking, a meta tag does nothing.*

3 · The nonce precedence law — one token de-fangs a whole directive

This is the law that breaks production apps. A policy carries 'unsafe-inline' to keep inline scripts working. Then somebody adds a nonce for security. One token. And every inline script that does not carry the nonce silently stops running.

Policyinline w/ nonceinline w/o nonceevent handlerVerdict
THE NONCE-KILLS-UNSAFE-INLINE LAW (headline). When a directive contains both a nonce (or hash) and 'unsafe-inline', the browser ignores 'unsafe-inline' entirely — for that whole directive, not just for the element carrying the nonce.
  • script-src 'unsafe-inline' → the inline script and the event handler both run
  • script-src 'unsafe-inline' 'nonce-x' → the nonced script runs, the plain inline script and the event handler die
  • script-src 'self' 'unsafe-inline' → both run
  • script-src 'self' 'unsafe-inline' 'nonce-x' → both die
*The order of the tokens does not matter — 'nonce-x' 'unsafe-inline' and 'unsafe-inline' 'nonce-x' behave identically. You cannot keep a legacy inline script alive by listing 'unsafe-inline' first. Measured across Chromium 155, Brave 154 and WebKit 26.5 — all three agree.*
THE HASH-DOES-NOT-COVER-HANDLERS LAW. A script-src 'sha256-…' policy that hashes an inline script authorises that script — measured, the hashed inline script ran and every other inline script died. But the same hash does not authorise an event handler: onclick="…" is governed by script-src-attr, and a hash in script-src does not reach it. The handler died in all three engines.
THE 'unsafe-hashes' LAW — the only way to keep a hashed handler. Adding the 'unsafe-hashes' source expression authorises a hashed event handler:
  • script-src 'sha256-…' → handler blocked
  • script-src 'unsafe-hashes' 'sha256-…' → handler allowed
  • script-src 'unsafe-hashes' 'sha256-wrong' → handler blocked (the hash must match)
  • script-src 'unsafe-hashes' 'nonce-x' 'sha256-…' → handler allowed and the nonced script allowed — the two mechanisms compose
⚠️ The sharp edge: the hash is computed over the attribute's exact text. The same handler written with one extra space is a different hash and is silently blocked. Measured with a padded handler: the correctly-hashed padded text ran, and the unpadded hash against the padded text did not. *A hash of an event handler is a tripwire — one reformat of your JSX and the click handler is dead with no error.*

4 · The strict-dynamic ladder — the policy that ignores its own allow-list

'strict-dynamic' is the modern replacement for an allow-list: it trusts scripts loaded by a trusted script, no matter where they came from. The surprise is how much it changes.

Policyinline w/ nonceparser external scriptdynamic inline scriptVerdict
THE STRICT-DYNAMIC LAW. Under script-src 'strict-dynamic' 'nonce-x':
  • the nonced inline script runs ✓
  • the parser-inserted external script is BLOCKED ✗ — even with 'self' in the same directive
  • the dynamic inline script (appended by JavaScript) runs ✓
*Adding 'self' to a strict-dynamic policy changes nothing for the parser-inserted script — it is blocked either way. That is the point: strict-dynamic inverts the model, so an allow-list in the same directive is dead weight.*
THE STRICT-DYNAMIC-NO-NONCE LAW. script-src 'strict-dynamic' with no nonce blocks everything — the inline script, the parser script, and the dynamic script. A strict-dynamic policy without a nonce is a policy that blocks all scripts. And a wrong nonce is the same as no nonce: the page is blank and the console shows a violation, but nothing tells you the nonce was the problem.
THE SCRIPT-SRC-ELEM LAW. When you write only script-src, the browser uses it for script-src-elem too — and reports the violation against script-src-elem. That is why the console says "Note that 'script-src-elem' was not explicitly set, so 'script-src' is used as a fallback". The same applies to script-src-attr for event handlers, and to worker-src for workers. *If you want a different rule for event handlers than for scripts, you must set script-src-attr explicitly.*

5 · The reporting tier — the split that silently kills your telemetry

This is the headline. Two directives report violations: the legacy report-uri and the modern report-to. They behave completely differently, and the modern one replaces the legacy one rather than adding to it.

Chromium 155 report-to DEAD

report-uri → 13 reports delivered in ~550 ms.
report-to → 0 reports over HTTP, 0 over HTTPS, after 25 s.
Both together → 0 reports at the legacy endpoint too.

Brave 154 report-to DEAD

Identical to Chromium, 3 repetitions each: report-uri → 13, report-to → 0, both → 0.

WebKit 26.5 report-to WORKS

report-uri → 10 reports delivered.
report-to → 10 reports delivered to an HTTPS endpoint, as application/csp-report JSON.

THE REPORT-TO SUPPRESSION LAW (headline #1). In Chromium and Brave, adding report-to to a policy that also has report-uri silently disables the legacy reporting. Measured side by side, 3 repetitions:
  • report-uri /report alone → 13 reports received
  • report-uri /report; report-to g → 0 reports received at the legacy endpoint
  • the violation events still fire (13 of 13) and ReportingObserver still sees all 13
So the browser is not failing — it is generating the report and then not delivering it. The report is produced, observable in-page, and dropped on the floor. *A team that "modernises" a policy by adding report-to loses every violation report in every Chrome user, sees no error, and concludes the site is clean.*
THE REPORT-EXISTS LAW — and the API that proves it. The report is generated, in every engine and in report-ONLY mode. Measured with a ReportingObserver:
  • new ReportingObserver(cb).observe({type:'securitypolicyviolation', buffered:true}) → 13 reports in Chromium, 12 in WebKit, in every lane — including report-uri-only and report-to-only
  • the report body carries blockedURL, disposition, effectiveDirective, originalPolicy, statusCode, sourceFile, lineNumber, columnNumber and a sample
  • buffered:true replays reports that fired before the observer was installed — otherwise you lose the violations that happened during page load, which is where most of them are
*⚠️ Brave is the exception: ReportingObserver is typeof 'function' and every type string is accepted, but the callback never fired once in any lane. Brave accepts the observer and delivers nothing. A CSP dashboard built on the Reporting API is blind on Brave.*
THE REPORT-ONLY LAW. Content-Security-Policy-Report-Only reports and never blocks — measured: with a report-only policy, every script ran (inline ✓, external ✓, handler ✓) while 13 violations were still generated and delivered. *This is the correct way to roll out a policy: ship it report-only, watch the report, then enforce. And it is the only mode in which a Brave deployment can see its violations at all — because Brave's legacy report-uri path does work.*

6 · The document-level directives — base-uri and form-action

Most CSP work is about resources. These two are about the document itself: where relative URLs point, and where a form may submit. They are measured in isolation, because each one changes the meaning of every other URL on the page.

PolicyResultVerdict
THE BASE-URI LAW. base-uri 'none' makes a <base href> inert: the browser ignores it and document.baseURI stays the document URL. Measured: with base-uri 'none' and a <base href="https://example.com/x/">, document.baseURI read back as the document URL; with the directive absent it read back as https://example.com/x/.
  • base-uri 'none' → base ignored, 1 violation fired
  • base-uri 'self' → base ignored (a cross-origin base is not 'self'), 1 violation fired
  • base-uri https://example.com/ → base applied, 0 violations
  • no directive → base applied, 0 violations
*This is the directive that stops an injected <base> from re-pointing every relative script URL at an attacker. It is one line of header and it is on exactly one path — the injection that matters.*
THE FORM-ACTION LAW — AND THE 'self' TRAP. form-action 'none' blocks the submit: measured, the page stayed on the form URL, no navigation happened, the target origin received zero requests, and one form-action violation fired. But look at the second row:
  • form-action 'none' → submit BLOCKED, 0 requests
  • form-action 'self' → submit ALSO BLOCKED — and this is the trap: the form's action pointed at a different port on the same host, which is a different origin, so 'self' did not cover it
  • no directive → submit ALLOWED, the request arrived
  • form-action 'none' http://127.0.0.1:* → submit ALLOWED
*'self' in CSP is origin-exact: scheme + host + port. A form posting to https://example.com from https://example.com:8443 is cross-origin, and form-action 'self' blocks it. The same surprise applies to connect-src and img-src.*

7 · The grammar — what the parser does with a broken policy

A policy is a string, and the parser is forgiving in ways that are not forgiving of you. A typo is not a syntax error — it is a different policy, and the difference is invisible until you read what the browser actually applied.

Policy stringWhat the browser appliedVerdict
THE MISSING-SPACE LAW (headline). script-src'none' — no space between the directive name and the source list — is parsed as the directive name script-src'none', an unknown directive. The browser ignores the entire policy: 0 violations, every script ran. Measured against script-src 'none' (same characters, one space) which blocked everything with 13 violations.
  • script-src'none' → policy ignored entirely, 0 violations
  • default-src'none' → ignored, 0 violations
  • style-src'none' → ignored, 0 violations
  • script-src\t'none' (a tab) → enforced, 13 violations — a tab is whitespace
  • script-src 'none' (trailing space) → enforced
*A single missing space turns a locked-down policy into no policy at all — and the page looks fine, because the failure mode of a working policy is a broken page, and the failure mode of a broken policy is a normal page. You will never notice.*
THE FIRST-DUPLICATE-WINS LAW. When a directive appears twice, the first occurrence wins and the second is ignored — not the last, and not the union.
  • script-src 'self'; script-src 'none' → 'self' applied (external script ran)
  • script-src 'none'; script-src 'self' → 'none' applied (external script blocked)
*Two deployment layers each appending a policy fragment — a framework default plus a CDN header, say — will silently drop whichever comes second. Order is a security parameter.*
THE BAD-TOKEN LAW — a bad token voids its own directive, not the policy. An unrecognised source expression makes its own directive invalid, and an invalid directive is dropped (it does not fall back to default-src, and it does not void the rest of the policy).
  • script-src 'self'; style-src 'bogus' → script-src still enforced (inline scripts blocked), style-src dropped
  • script-src 'self'; img-src 'bogus' → script-src enforced; the image was blocked (an invalid directive falls back to default-src when one exists, and to "block" when none does)
  • totally-made-up 'self' alone → the whole policy is inert, 0 violations
*The asymmetric outcome is the trap: a typo in style-src silently leaves your styles unprotected while every script check still looks fine.*
THE EMPTY-DIRECTIVE LAW. script-src with no source list means 'none' — it is not "unset". Measured: script-src alone blocked every script (13 violations). *A directive with an empty value is the strictest policy you can write, and it is one keystroke away from a typo that removes it.*

8 · The style tier and the wasm tier — two directives with their own rules

style-src / style-src-attr / style-src-elem

style-src 'none' blocked both the <style> element and the style="" attribute. Splitting the directive changes it: style-src-attr 'none' alone blocked the attribute, and style-src-elem 'none' alone blocked the element. A style-src hash authorises a <style> element but not a style="" attribute — same shape as the script-src/script-src-attr split.

'wasm-unsafe-eval' ≠ 'unsafe-eval'

Under script-src 'self' 'wasm-unsafe-eval': eval() was blocked (EvalError) while WebAssembly.compile() and WebAssembly.instantiate() worked. 'unsafe-eval' allows both. They are separate permissions.

THE WASM LAW. A policy of script-src 'self' 'wasm-unsafe-eval' permitted WebAssembly.instantiate() (measured: a module exporting f() returned 42) while eval() threw EvalError. Adding 'unsafe-eval' permitted both. *For an app that ships a WASM decoder (a video codec, a WASM build of a JS library), 'wasm-unsafe-eval' is the narrow grant — it does not open eval().*
THE WORKER-LAW. A blob: worker is governed by worker-src, falling back to child-src, then script-src, then default-src. Under script-src 'none' the blob worker was blocked and the report said "Note that 'worker-src' was not explicitly set, so 'script-src' is used as a fallback". A policy that tightens script-src without adding worker-src breaks every worker you load from a blob — a common pattern for bundlers.

9 · The capstone — a CSP auditor for a live deployment

The laws above assemble into a tool. Paste a policy, and the auditor tells you what will break, in the order it will break it. This is the OpenDrones / Molt deliverable: a policy is a deployment artifact, and it deserves a pre-flight check like any other.

—
THE DELIVERABLE. A CSP auditor is a pre-flight check: parse the policy, apply the measured laws, and report (a) what breaks, (b) which reporting directive actually works in the browsers you care about, (c) whether the policy can be tightened without breaking the app. For a client site that is a half-day line item; for OpenDrones' operator console — which serves live drone telemetry over a WebSocket and a video downlink — it is the difference between a deployment that works and a cockpit that silently stops receiving frames.
