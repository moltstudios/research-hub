Timers & the Event-Loop Tier

The oldest API on the platform, still full of unexplored engine laws. This tier sits under every pipeline we ship — polling loops, animations, retries, debounces, heartbeats. Measured on Chromium (Chrome for Testing 148, headless, M-series) with cross-checks in dedicated workers; every claim on this page re-probes in your engine on load.

THE HEADLINE FIVE. ① setTimeout(fn,0) fires in 0.0–0.1 ms at shallow nesting — the “0 means 4 ms” folklore only applies past nesting level 5. ② A hidden tab clamps timer gaps from ~10 ms to ≥1000 ms — but a dedicated worker’s timers are exempt. ③ Missed intervals are skipped, never burst: one late fire, then realignment to the original grid. ④ setTimeout(fn, 2³¹) fires immediately — the delay field overflows to 0 at 2 147 483 648. ⑤ In the same event-loop turn, rAF and rIC run BEFORE the 0-ms timeouts — and the timer queue is delay-sorted (a later-armed 0 ms can fire after an earlier-armed 1 ms).

Sections 1–10 are live labs. Section 11 carries the OpenDrones + Molt capstones; section 12 is the decision table.

§1 · Surface census

Arity facts: setTimeout.length === 1 (only the handler is required — timeout has a default), requestIdleCallback.length === 1 (the options bag is optional). setImmediate never shipped outside Node — it is undefined here.

THE WORKER SPLIT. A dedicated worker has setTimeout/setInterval/queueMicrotask — but no requestIdleCallback (undefined), while requestAnimationFrame exists and fires (§8). Idle-scheduling code must not assume the idle lane exists off-main-thread.

§2 · THE NESTING CLAMP — chained timer lab

Two chains armed from a clean task (a MessageChannel task, so no inherited nesting), re-arming themselves: one at delay 0, one at delay 1. Bars = measured per-hop gap (ms). Orange = clamped hop.

runs live — ~80 ms
THE CLAMP LAW. Hops 1–5 dispatch at their nominal delay (0 ms measured 0.0–0.1 ms; 1 ms ≈ 1.2–1.4 ms). From nesting level 6 on, any delay < 4 ms is raised to 4 ms — measured as a ~5 ms steady floor on this engine. A fresh timer armed from a non-timer task is never clamped, no matter how many timers you arm at once (a 12-at-once batch all fired at 1.3 ms — the “parallel timers slow down” folklore is a measurement artifact, see §4 note).

Practical: animation loops that re-arm setTimeout(fn, 0) silently degrade from sub-millisecond to 4 ms cadence after 5 laps. If you need tight loops, either stay at ≥ 4 ms honestly or move to MessageChannel (§4).

§3 · Delay conversion grammar — truncation, garbage, and the 2³¹ cliff

THE TRUNCATION LAW. The fractional part of the delay is dropped, not rounded: min-of-7 fire for 200.9 is ~200.4 ms — it fires at the 200 ms deadline. (The “rounds to nearest” folklore — including several books — is falsified by the minimum-fire lane; median/mean readings hide this under dispatch overhead.) Safe habit: pass integer delays.
THE 2³¹ CLIFF. setTimeout(fn, 2147483647) is a real 24.8-day timer. One more — 2147483648 — fires immediately: the delay overflows the signed 32-bit field and lands at 0. This is the classic “my 25-day lease-renewal timeout ran instantly” bug. Any delay ≥ 2³¹ (including Infinity, NaN, negatives, and 1e308) fires at the 0-lane.

String delays coerce: setTimeout(fn, '10') = 10 ms. Everything else numeric-but-invalid (−500, NaN, Infinity) → 0-lane (~0–5 ms fire).

§4 · THE ORDERING CENSUS — one task schedules everything

~1 frame
THE TURN GRAMMAR (measured, Chromium). Microtasks first, in queue order — queueMicrotask and Promise.then interleave FIFO, they are the same queue. Then the next task: MessageChannel before timers (stable task-source priority). rAF and rIC dispatch at the frame boundary — whether they beat same-turn timeouts is a frame-deadline race: both orders were measured on this engine (rAF before timeouts in one run, after them in the next — a frame must land inside the window). The 0/1/0 timeouts fire delay-sorted: 0a, 1, 0b — the second 0 ms armed AFTER the 1 ms still fires last among them. The timer queue is a priority queue by deadline with FIFO tiebreak, not a FIFO.

Folklore falsified twice: “setTimeout(0) beats rAF” (no — the frame runs first) and “timers fire in arm order” (no — in deadline order). Note for §2’s artifact: if you arm timers from a continuation whose promise was resolved inside a deep timer callback, your new timer inherits that nesting level — a 40-hop chain poisons a whole await sleep() lineage to the 4 ms clamp. Continuations of promises resolved by non-timer tasks (MessageChannel, events) inherit nothing.

§5 · Timer IDs & the clear() that never throws

THE SHARED MONOTONIC ID LAW. setTimeout and setInterval draw from one counter: interleaved calls give consecutive integers (107, 108, 109 measured). clearTimeout cancels an interval ID and clearInterval cancels a timeout ID — the pair are interchangeable. IDs are never reused after mass clears (next-fresh continues past the last cleared). And clearTimeout() accepts anything — 0, -1, 1.7, 'abc', null, {}, NaN, Infinity — all silent, no TypeError. There is no feedback channel: clearing a fired or never-existed timer is indistinguishable from success.

§6 · THE SKIP LAW — what an interval does when you block

THE SKIP LAW. The tick due during the block fires once, late (at ~540 ms instead of 180); the ~6 ticks that came due meanwhile are skipped — no burst, no catch-up — and the next fires realign to the original grid (600.3, 660.3 …). If you need “make up missed work,” you must diff wall-clock yourself; the interval engine silently drops backlog. (Contrast: at interval(1) the steady-state gap floor is the same 4/5 ms nesting clamp — intervals are chained timers internally.)

THE 1 HZ LAW + THE WORKER EXEMPTION. The moment document.visibilityState === 'hidden', every main-thread timer lane clamps to ≥ 1000 ms gaps (a 10 ms heartbeat becomes 1 Hz — a 100× telemetry slowdown for a Mission Control tab sitting in the background). Timers armed while hidden get the 1 s minimum from birth. rAF and rIC fire zero times while hidden. The dedicated worker’s timer chain keeps its ~10 ms cadence unchanged — workers are exempt from visibility throttling. That is the entire architecture of the §11 heartbeat capstone.

After 5 minutes continuously hidden, Chromium goes further: intensive throttling aligns wake-ups to ≤ 1/min (with a CPU-budget exemption for pages playing audio, holding WebRTC, or with active USB/serial). That tier is documented in Chromium’s “Intensive Wake Up Throttling” spec — the first-stage 1 s clamp above is verifiable live in your own tab right now; the ≤ 1/min tier needs 5 minutes hidden to observe. Budget-backed lanes (audio, USB) reset the counter — relevant for any ground-station page holding a serial port open. Harness note: Playwright launches Chrome with --disable-background-timer-throttling --disable-backgrounding-occluded-windows --disable-renderer-backgrounding by default — background-tier claims made from Playwright probes without ignoreDefaultArgs are measuring a throttling-disabled browser.

§8 · rAF in a worker — the phantom that isn’t

THE WORKER-RAF LAW. In a dedicated worker with no OffscreenCanvas at all, requestAnimationFrame is present and fires — measured one-shot and looping. It is the frame clock without a document. Meanwhile requestIdleCallback is absent in workers entirely. The scheduling surface is not a subset of the page’s — it’s a different set.

THE DRIFT LAW. Every interval fire lands a little late (dispatch + clamp), and the error accumulates — raw interval(100) loses seconds per minute while the corrected chain stays within one lap of wall time forever. Any countdown, ETA, or rate display built on a raw interval lies progressively. Recompute against an absolute clock (the right-hand pattern) — never trust tick counts as time.

§10 · Callback grammar — this, args, strings, validation

THE THIS LAW (folklore falsified). Timer callbacks run with this === window in sloppy mode — and also in strict mode (verified with a script-injected strict IIFE; the HTML spec passes the global as the callback this-value unconditionally). The common “strict mode makes timer this undefined” claim is wrong on this engine. Pass args or bind — never rely on this-binding. String handlers still evaluate (the eval family — blocked under CSP without unsafe-eval); queueMicrotask(undefined) throws a TypeError verbatim.

§11 · Capstones

OpenDrones — the background heartbeat

Mission Control must keep telemetry flowing when the pilot’s tab is in the background. The 1 Hz clamp kills any main-thread poll — so own the clock in a worker:

// heartbeat.js (dedicated worker)
let next = performance.now();
(function beat(){
  next += 100;                       // absolute grid: no drift
  setTimeout(beat, Math.max(0, next - performance.now()));
  postMessage(tick());               // → page, even while hidden
})();
// page: no timers at all — just onmessage

Worker timers are visibility-exempt (§7), the chain never drifts (§9), and the 4 ms clamp is moot at a 100 ms cadence (§2). Same pattern for the watchdog: an in-worker supervisor notices main-thread silence — because the page’s rAF stopped — without owning a page timer.

Molt — clamp-aware UX code

  • Debounce: trailing debounces below 4 ms silently degrade under nesting — floor UI debounces at 4 ms+ (or use MessageChannel for sub-ms, §4).
  • Countdowns: never count ticks; snapshot performance.now() and render against wall clock (§9).
  • Idle work: rIC exists only on main-thread Chromium — pair with a setTimeout(…, 0) fallback; remember rIC never fires in hidden tabs (§7).
  • Retry/backoff: keep delays under 2³¹−1 and finite — the overflow fires immediately (§3), turning “retry tomorrow” into a hot loop.
  • Order assumptions: if ordering matters between a timeout and a postMessage/rAF, don’t rely on arm order — the queue is delay-sorted (§4).

Ten rules.

  • A 0 ms timer at shallow nesting is sub-millisecond — don’t “optimize” it away out of fear of the 4 ms folklore (§2).
  • Nesting is per-chain: re-arming from within a timer callback at depth > 5 clamps you to 4 ms (§2).
  • Promises resolved inside a deep timer callback pass that nesting to your continuation (§4 note).
  • Delay fractions truncate — 200.9 fires at the 200 deadline (§3).
  • Negative / NaN / Infinity / ≥ 2³¹ all mean “now” (§3).
  • clear* never throws and returns nothing — track your own handles (§5).
  • Intervals skip: one late fire, grid realign, backlog dropped (§6).
  • Hidden tab = 1 Hz main-thread, 0 rAF/rIC, worker unharmed (§7).
  • Tick counts are not time — always diff against performance.now() (§9).
  • this is window even in strict mode; string args are eval — use functions (§10).
